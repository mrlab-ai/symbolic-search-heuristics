% ============================== abstract =================================
\begin{abstract}
Heuristics guide explicit \astar\ search well but often fail to pay off in symbolic search, which represents sets of states as binary decision diagrams (BDDs).
We analyze why for forward symbolic \astar, which splits every layer of the search into buckets of states with the same heuristic value.
We factor the number of BDD nodes it expands into pruning, which removes states and thus shrinks the BDDs of the layers, and fragmentation, the cost of representing each layer by one BDD per bucket.
In experiments on \num{\HoldoutTasks} planning tasks, we find that BDDs shrink much less than the state sets they represent, so harmful heuristics barely shrink the layers and fragment them instead, and heuristics pay off mainly where they remove most states.
The same holds for operator potentials in the state-of-the-art search GHSETA*, which pay off overall.
Avoiding fragmentation would help much more often, but it needs a tight bound on the optimal plan cost, which neither iterative deepening nor a fast satisficing planner provides cheaply.
Finally, we prove that the cofactor width of a heuristic bounds both effects and that the bound on fragmentation is tight up to a constant factor, although these worst-case bounds do not predict fragmentation in practice.
\end{abstract}

% ============================ introduction ===============================
\section{Introduction}

Cost-optimal classical planners commonly use \astar\ \citep{hart-et-al-ieeessc1968} with an admissible heuristic that estimates the goal distance of individual states \citep{pearl-1984}.
Symbolic search instead expands sets of states, which it represents as binary decision diagrams \citep[BDDs;][]{bryant-ieeecomp1986,mcmillan-1993,edelkamp-helmert-ecp1999}.
Blind symbolic search is among the strongest approaches for cost-optimal planning \citep{torralba-et-al-aij2017,speck-ipc2023,speck-et-al-jair2025}.
Heuristics, however, help symbolic search much less than explicit search.
Symbolic variants of \astar\ such as \bddastar\ \citep{edelkamp-reffel-ki1998}, SetA\ensuremath{^{*}} \citep{jensen-et-al-aaai2002}, \ghseta\ \citep{jensen-et-al-aij2008} and ADDA\ensuremath{^{*}} \citep{hansen-et-al-sara2002} help in some domains and hurt in others \citep{torralba-phd2015,torralba-et-al-aij2017}.
\citet{speck-et-al-icaps2020} even show that the perfect heuristic \hstar\ can increase the size of the expanded BDDs exponentially, so heuristic accuracy alone cannot explain the effect of a heuristic on symbolic search.

We explain this effect by separating two roles that a heuristic plays in symbolic \astar.
As in explicit search, the heuristic prunes states whose $f$-value exceeds the optimal plan cost.
In addition, it partitions the remaining states of every layer into buckets with the same heuristic value, and symbolic search represents each bucket by its own BDD.
Since the BDD of a subset can be much larger than the BDD of its superset, the buckets of a layer can together need many more nodes than the layer.
We therefore factor the number of expanded BDD nodes into the BDD size of the expanded layers without the partition, which pruning reduces, and a \emph{fragmentation factor}, which measures the cost of the partition.

With this factorization, we analyze pattern database (PDB), merge-and-shrink (M\&S) and potential heuristics in the symbolic planner SymK \citep{speck-et-al-jair2025} on \num{\HoldoutTasks} IPC tasks, with the protocol and the primary analyses of each study fixed before its experiments.
Whenever one of these heuristics expands more than twice as many BDD nodes as blind search, it barely prunes and fragments the layers instead, and heuristics pay off mainly on tasks where they remove most states.
Even a heuristic that removes half of the states can leave the BDDs almost as large as before, since BDDs shrink much less than the state sets they represent.
The same diagnosis holds for stronger heuristics, such as potentials optimized for all states, M\&S with \num{200000} states and operator potentials in GHSETA* \citep{fiser-et-al-aij2024}: they help where they remove most states and fragment the layers elsewhere.
Operator potentials solve significantly more tasks than blind search, forward (\CpSolvedPotFw\ versus \CpSolvedBlindFw) and bidirectionally (\CpSolvedPotBi\ versus \CpSolvedBlindBi), but strong potentials in SymK decompose almost identically and gain much less, so the decomposition does not explain the size of this gain.

Without fragmentation, heuristics would pay off much more often: a search that knew the optimal plan cost and pruned by $f$-value without splitting the layers would expand fewer BDD nodes than blind search on \OracleAllUnsplitBelow\ of \num{\GenAllPairs} pairs of heuristic and task, compared with \OracleAllEffortBelow\ for symbolic \astar.
Forward search knows no such bound, however.
Using heuristics only to prune dead ends removes the fragmentation but almost all pruning with it, and finding the bound by iterative deepening costs more than the fragmentation it avoids.
With the plan cost of the satisficing planner LAMA as bound, heuristics no longer hurt, but this bound is too loose to prune much, and blind search with the same bound does as well.
For the heuristics of our main studies in SymK, no forward use solves significantly more tasks than blind forward search, and blind bidirectional search solves the most tasks.

To understand how much a heuristic can fragment the search, we bound fragmentation by the \emph{cofactor width} of the heuristic, the largest number of distinct residual functions of the heuristic at any cut of the BDD variable order.
Cofactor width is the standard width of a multi-terminal BDD, and our bounds build on the standard product construction \citep{wegener-2000}.
We connect both to the buckets of symbolic search: for a consistent heuristic with $V$ finite values and cofactor width $W$ on $n$ state bits, fragmentation and the number of expanded BDD nodes relative to blind search are at most $2VWn$, and a family of polynomial-size planning tasks with $n\geq8W$ attains $VWn/120$.
Hence, only heuristics of exponential width can increase the effort exponentially, such as the perfect heuristic in the construction of \citet{speck-et-al-icaps2020}, whereas the width of PDBs and of M\&S heuristics with a suitable merge order is at most linear in their abstraction size.
For the PDBs of our PDB study, a cut-wise refinement of the bound exceeds the observed fragmentation by a median factor of \SlackProfileMedian, but it does not predict how fragmentation changes under other variable orders.
Since pruning also keeps a subset of each layer selected by heuristic value, the same argument shows that removing states need not shrink the BDDs, but the bounds do not predict how much a given heuristic prunes.

% ============================ background =================================
\section{Background}

We consider finite deterministic SAS$^{+}$ tasks $\task=\langle\vars,\ops,\sinit,\sgoal\rangle$ \citep{backstrom-nebel-compint1995} with positive integer operator costs.
The functions $\gstar(s)$ and $\hstar(s)$ give the cheapest costs from \sinit\ to $s$ and from $s$ to a goal, respectively, with value $\infty$ if no such path exists.
We write $\copt=\hstar(\sinit)$ for the optimal plan cost and assume $0<\copt<\infty$.
A heuristic $h\colon\states\to\mathbb Z_{\geq0}\cup\{\infty\}$ is \emph{admissible} if $h\leq\hstar$, and \emph{consistent} if $h(s)\leq\cost(o)+h(s[o])$ for every applicable operator $o$ and $h=0$ on goals.
The blind heuristic is $\hblind\equiv0$.

\paragraph{Binary Decision Diagrams.}
We encode every SAS$^{+}$ variable $v$ with $\lceil\log_2|D_v|\rceil$ Boolean bits and write $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ for all bits in a fixed variable order $\pi$.
The order is \emph{variable-contiguous} if the bits of each SAS$^{+}$ variable are consecutive.
Some bit strings encode no state, so every heuristic receives a total extension to all of $\{0,1\}^n$; our upper bounds apply to the chosen extension, and lower bounds witnessed on bit strings that encode states apply to every extension.
The supplement fixes the extensions for which our width bounds for common heuristics hold, for example value $0$ for the invalid codes of a PDB.
As in the CUDD library that SymK uses, a state set $S$ is represented by the reduced ordered BDD of its characteristic function $\chi_S$ with complemented edges, and $\bddsize{S}$ counts its regular inner nodes \citep{bryant-ieeecomp1986}.
For a function $f$ and a prefix $\rho\in\{0,1\}^i$ of the bits, $f|_\rho$ denotes the residual function, or \emph{cofactor}, of $f$, and $\sub{i}{f}=\{f|_\rho\mid\rho\in\{0,1\}^i\}$ is the set of distinct cofactors at cut $i$, where $f$ and $\neg f$ count as distinct.
We write $\cutcount{i}{f}=|\sub{i}{f}|$.

\begin{definition}[cofactor count]
\label{def-cofcount}
For a function $f$ over $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the \emph{cofactor count} is $\cofcount(f) = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \cutcount{i}{f}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem-qsize}
For every Boolean $f$ over $x_1, \dots, x_n$, $\bddsize{f} \leq \cofcount(f) \leq n \cdot (\bddsize{f} + 2)$.
If $f$ is nonconstant, also $\cofcount(f)\leq2n\bddsize{f}$.
\end{lemma}

The cofactor count is the number of nodes of the quasi-reduced BDD of $f$, counting both polarities (supplement).
The factor $n$ is unavoidable already for $f=x_1$, where $\cofcount(f)=2n-1$ but $\bddsize{f}=1$.

\paragraph{Symbolic Search.}
Symbolic uniform-cost search represents each layer $L_g=\{s\mid\gstar(s)=g\}$ of states with cheapest cost $g$ by one BDD \citep{torralba-et-al-aij2017}.
Forward \bddastar\ \citep{edelkamp-reffel-ki1998} additionally intersects the layers with the level sets $\level{h}{v}=\{s\mid h(s)=v\}$ of the heuristic, and we consider the product-at-evaluation variant \citep{speck-et-al-icaps2020}.
It discards states with $h=\infty$, represents each bucket of states with $g$-value $g$ and $h$-value $v$ by one BDD and selects buckets by increasing $(f,g)=(g+v,g)$.
Before it expands a bucket, it removes the states that are already closed with their cheapest $g$-value, and below, ``bucket'' means this remaining set.
The search tests for a goal when it selects a bucket and moves the entire bucket to the closed list before generating its successors.
These assumptions about the queue, duplicates, goal tests and positive costs define the scope of our bounds.

\begin{definition}[expansion size, \citealp{speck-et-al-icaps2020}]
\label{def-effort}
Let $B_{\task,h}(g,v)$ denote the BDD that \bddastar\ expands with $g$-value $g$ and $h$-value $v$ on task \task\ with heuristic $h$, or the empty set if no such expansion occurs.
For consistent $h$, the \emph{expansion size}, or \emph{effort}, of \bddastar\ is $\effort(\task,h) = \sum_{\,g + v \leq \copt,\ g < \copt} |B_{\task,h}(g,v)|$.
\end{definition}

Lemma~\ref{lem-identity} below shows that exactly these nonempty buckets are expanded before the search terminates.
With $\hblind$, \bddastar\ is uniform-cost search and $\effort(\task,\hblind) = \sum_{g < \copt} \bddsize{L_g}$.
\citet{speck-et-al-icaps2020} show that on some tasks even \hstar\ has exponentially larger effort than blind search.

% ===================== pruning versus fragmentation ======================
\section{Pruning and Fragmentation}

A heuristic changes the effort of symbolic search in two ways.
It prunes states whose $f$-value exceeds \copt, which reduces the expanded part of each layer, and it splits that part into several buckets.
For a completed search, let $A_g(h)$ be the set of $h$-values whose nonempty buckets are expanded with $g$-value $g$, and let $S_g^h=\bigcup_{v\in A_g(h)}B_{\task,h}(g,v)$ be the expanded part of layer $g$.
We define the \emph{unsplit effort} and the \emph{fragmentation factor} as
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \unioneffort(\task,h)&=\textstyle\sum_g\bddsize{S_g^h},\\
 \fragfactor(\task,h)&=\effort(\task,h)/\unioneffort(\task,h),
 \end{aligned}
\]
whenever the denominator is positive.
By definition, $\effort=\unioneffort\cdot\fragfactor$ (Figure~\ref{fig:factorization}).
The unsplit effort measures the expanded states as if each layer were represented by one BDD, and the fragmentation factor measures the cost of splitting them by heuristic value.
Blind search has one bucket per layer, so $\fragfactor(\task,\hblind)=1$.
The fragmentation factor can be smaller than one, since the BDD of a union can be larger than the BDDs of its disjoint parts together.

To compare a heuristic with blind search on the same task, we use the \emph{effort ratio} $r=\effort(\task,h)/\effort(\task,\hblind)$ and the \emph{unsplit ratio} $u=\unioneffort(\task,h)/\effort(\task,\hblind)$.
Since blind search does not fragment, $r=u\cdot\fragfactor$: a heuristic reduces effort only if its pruning ($u<1$) outweighs its fragmentation.
The two effects also act on different layers.
By Lemma~\ref{lem-identity} below, \bddastar\ expands exactly the states of layer $g$ with $h\leq\copt-g$, so a heuristic with largest finite value $h_{\max}$ prunes only dead ends in the layers $g\leq\copt-h_{\max}$, whereas it can fragment every layer.

% ===================== width-bounded heuristics ==========================
\section{Cofactor Width}

To bound fragmentation, we need a property of the heuristic that controls the BDD size of every subset it selects by value.
We use the number of distinct cofactors of the heuristic at each cut.
The supplement contains full proofs of all results in this and the following section.

\begin{definition}[cofactor width]
\label{def-width}
The \emph{cofactor width} of a heuristic $h$ under variable order $\pi$ is $\width_\pi(h) = \max_{0 \leq i \leq n} \cutcount{i}{h}$.
We call $h$ \emph{$(\pi,W)$-width-bounded} if $\width_\pi(h) \leq W$, and we write \emph{width} for cofactor width.
\end{definition}

The cofactors of $h$ are the states of a layered deterministic automaton that reads the bits in order $\pi$, so width is the largest level of the quasi-reduced multi-terminal BDD (MTBDD) of $h$ \citep[Thm.~3.2.2; Sec.~9.2]{wegener-2000}.
Width is thus a standard representation property applied to heuristics, and it is unrelated to novelty width in planning \citep{lipovetzky-geffner-ecai2012}.
Width bounds the number of heuristic values, since the cofactors at the last cut are the attained constants.
We write $\values=|h(\states)\setminus\{\infty\}|$ for the number of finite values on planning states and $T=|\hrange(h)|$ for the number of values of the total extension, so $\values\leq T\leq W$.
Since a cofactor of a combined heuristic is determined by a pair of cofactors of its operands, the width of a sum, maximum or minimum of two heuristics is at most the product of their widths.
Mapping the values of an admissible and consistent estimate by a nondecreasing function $\phi$ with $\phi(0)=0$, $\phi(\infty)=\infty$ and $\phi(a+c)\leq\phi(a)+c$ for all $c\geq0$, such as rectification $\max(0,q)$ or a cap $\min(\max(0,q),\kappa)$, preserves admissibility and consistency and never increases width.
Width and the size of the reduced ordered algebraic decision diagram (ADD) of the heuristic \citep{bahar-et-al-iccad1993} bound each other up to a factor $n$ and an additive $|\hrange(h)|$ when both are measured under the same variable order, which must be the order of the search \citep{kissmann-hoffmann-jair2014}.
The supplement proves these properties.

\paragraph{Common Heuristics.}
Under a variable-contiguous order, three common families have bounded width (supplement).
A pattern database \citep[PDB;][]{culberson-schaeffer-compint1998,edelkamp-ecp2001} for a pattern $P$ has width at most the number $|\states^P|$ of its abstract states.
A merge-and-shrink (M\&S) heuristic \citep{helmert-et-al-jacm2014} with a linear merge order that agrees with the BDD order has width at most $d(N+1)$, where $d$ is the largest domain size and $N$ bounds the outputs of every table in its cascade \citep{helmert-et-al-icaps2015}.
A rectified potential heuristic \citep{pommerening-et-al-aaai2015} with integer potentials in $[-M,M]$ on $m$ variables has width at most $d(2mM+1)$.
We give no analogous bound for $h^{\max}$, $h^{\mathrm{add}}$ or LM-cut, but their width can be measured after construction.

% ======================= fragmentation theorems ==========================
\section{Bounding Pruning and Fragmentation}
\label{sec-fragmentation}

Fix $\pi$ and write $W=\width_\pi(h)$.
The key observation is that, at every cut, the cofactor of a subset selected by heuristic value is determined by the pair of the cofactor of the state set and the cofactor of the heuristic.
For a state set $S$, the \emph{cofactor-profile product} therefore multiplies the two cofactor counts cut by cut:
\[
  \cutprod{S}{h}=\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \cutcount{i}{\chi_S}\cutcount{i}{h}\leq W\cofcount(\chi_S).
\]

\begin{theorem}[bucket bound]
\label{thm-bucket}
Let $S \subseteq \{0,1\}^n$ and $U \subseteq \hrange(h)$.
Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\bddsize{S \cap h^{-1}(U)}
  &\leq \cofcount(\chi_{S \cap h^{-1}(U)}) \leq \cutprod{S}{h} \\
  &\leq W\cofcount(\chi_S) \leq Wn(\bddsize{S}+2).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $F=S\cap h^{-1}(U)$ and let $\rho$ be a prefix of length $i$.
The cofactor $\chi_F|_\rho$ maps a suffix $\sigma$ to $1$ if and only if $\chi_S|_\rho(\sigma)=1$ and $h|_\rho(\sigma)\in U$, so it is determined by the pair $(\chi_S|_\rho,h|_\rho)$.
Hence, $\cutcount{i}{\chi_F}\leq\cutcount{i}{\chi_S}\cutcount{i}{h}$, and summing over all cuts gives $\cofcount(\chi_F)\leq\cutprod{S}{h}$.
The remaining inequalities follow from Lemma~\ref{lem-qsize} and $\cutcount{i}{h}\leq W$.
\end{proof}

The theorem covers single buckets ($U=\{v\}$) as well as pruning by a threshold, and the factor $n$ is needed.

\begin{example}
\label{ex-parity}
Let $S=\{x_1=1\}$ and let $h=x_1\oplus x_2\oplus\dots\oplus x_n$ be a parity heuristic with values $0$ and $1$.
The BDD of $S$ has one node, but keeping only its states with $h=0$, which removes half of them, yields a BDD with $\Theta(n)$ nodes even with complemented edges.
\end{example}

To lift the bound to complete searches, we characterize exactly which buckets \bddastar\ expands.

\begin{lemma}[bucket identity]
\label{lem-identity}
Let $h$ be consistent.
\bddastar\ on \task\ with $h$ selects every bucket key $(g,v)$ with $g + v \leq \copt$, $g < \copt$ and $L_g \cap \level{h}{v} \neq \emptyset$ exactly once before terminating, and the BDD expanded with key $(g,v)$ represents exactly $L_g \cap \level{h}{v}$.
No other bucket is expanded before termination.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Consistency makes the $f$-values of generated successors nondecreasing, and with positive costs and ties broken by increasing $g$, every predecessor that could add states to a key is selected first, so no selected key receives new states.
Induction along cheapest paths gives cheapest $g$-values and complete buckets, and an open prefix of an optimal plan prevents selection above \copt.
\end{proof}

Hence, the expanded part $S_g^h=L_g\cap h^{-1}(\{v\mid v\leq\copt-g\})$ of each layer and each of its buckets are subsets selected by heuristic value, and Theorem~\ref{thm-bucket} bounds both.
Summing over the buckets of the expanded layers gives the \emph{profile bound}
\[
 \fragfactor(\task,h)\leq\profilescore(\task,h)=
 \frac{\sum_g|A_g(h)|\,\cutprod{S_g^h}{h}}{\unioneffort(\task,h)},
\]
which can only be computed after the search, whereas width relaxes it to a property of the heuristic alone.

\begin{theorem}[pruning and fragmentation bounds]
\label{thm-effort}
Let $h$ be consistent with $\width_\pi(h)\leq W$ and $\values$ finite values on planning states.
Then $\unioneffort(\task,h)\leq2Wn\cdot\effort(\task,\hblind)$, $\fragfactor(\task,h)\leq\profilescore(\task,h)\leq2\values Wn$ and $\effort(\task,h)\leq2\values Wn\cdot\effort(\task,\hblind)\leq2W^2n\cdot\effort(\task,\hblind)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Every nonempty layer below \copt\ and every expanded set contains no goal state, so Lemma~\ref{lem-qsize} gives $\cofcount(\chi_S)\leq2n\bddsize{S}$ for these sets.
With Theorem~\ref{thm-bucket} and at most $\values$ buckets per layer, all three bounds follow.
\end{proof}

The bounds are one-sided, since a heuristic can still reduce effort arbitrarily by pruning, and they concern the expanded BDDs of forward product-at-evaluation \bddastar, not the relational products of SetA\ensuremath{^{*}} or \ghseta\ and not bidirectional search.
The bounds on fragmentation and effort are tight up to a constant factor in the worst case.
For all $2\leq\values\leq W$ and arbitrarily large numbers $n\geq8W$ of state bits, the supplement constructs polynomial-size tasks with consistent heuristics of width $W$ and $\values$ values that prune nothing but attain a fragmentation factor and effort ratio of at least $\values Wn/120$.
Removing states guarantees no smaller BDD either: Example~\ref{ex-parity} halves $S$ but enlarges its BDD.

The perfect heuristic shows that large width is needed for large harm.
On the tasks of \citet{speck-et-al-icaps2020}, whose perfect heuristic increases the effort exponentially, \hstar\ has width at least $2^k$ under their order, where $k$ is their size parameter (supplement): at the cut between their two blocks of $k$ variables, every subset of the first block yields a distinct cofactor.
By Theorem~\ref{thm-effort}, a heuristic of polynomial width cannot increase the effort exponentially, so exponential width is necessary for such an increase, although it is not sufficient.

% ============================ experiments ================================
\section{Experiments}
\label{sec-experiments}

Our experiments answer four questions.
Which of the two effects explains when a heuristic hurts symbolic search, and does the answer hold for stronger heuristics (Q1)?
Do heuristics pay off once they no longer fragment the layers (Q2)?
Does the diagnosis explain why operator potentials in GHSETA* pay off (Q3)?
How tight are our bounds, and do they predict fragmentation (Q4)?

\paragraph{Setup.}
All experiments use SymK \citep{speck-et-al-jair2025} with forward product-at-evaluation \bddastar\ and a fixed, task-specific BDD variable order, the order of the planner Gamer \citep{kissmann-edelkamp-aaai2011} unless stated otherwise.
SymK selects buckets by increasing $(f,g)$ and tests for goals at selection, as Lemma~\ref{lem-identity} assumes.
We use the optimal sequential IPC tasks and remove 290 tasks with zero-cost operators, which Lemma~\ref{lem-identity} excludes, and 30 tasks with axioms, which leaves \num{\CapFullTasks} of \num{1697} tasks from \CapFullDomains\ domains.
Each run is limited to 300 seconds of CPU time and 8\,GiB of memory, shorter than the 30 minutes of the IPC, so that we can afford many configurations and repeated profiled runs; we report runs that fail while constructing the heuristic separately.
After using 50 tasks for a screening study of PDB selection, we split the remaining \num{\CapPrimaryTasks} tasks into a domain-balanced development set of 275 tasks, on which we developed all measurements, and a disjoint holdout set of \num{\HoldoutTasks} tasks from \HoldoutDomains\ domains, on which we report all results.
For each study, we fixed the protocol, the primary analysis and the decision criteria before inspecting any holdout result; the supplement gives them with the detailed results, and we mark all post-hoc analyses.
Table~\ref{tab:studies} lists the studies.

\paragraph{Heuristics.}
The \emph{PDB study}, designed to evaluate PDB selection by width, runs blind search and 16 PDB selectors that limit cofactor width to $K\in\{2,4,8,16,32\}$ without or with value caps, limit ADD size at five matching budgets or impose no limit (\num{\HoldoutCells} runs).
The \emph{family study} runs blind search and five heuristics from three families (\num{\GenCells} runs).
Three are M\&S heuristics: two with \num{10000} abstract states and a merge strategy that does or does not agree with the BDD order, and one with \num{50000} states and a merge strategy that does not.
The other two are a single PDB whose pattern CEGAR selects within \num{1000000} abstract states and rectified potentials with integer weights $P(v,w)\in[-4,4]$ for every fact $v=w$, optimized for the initial state by a mixed-integer program that CPLEX solves; the cap keeps their width small.
Both studies record the expanded buckets and the unsplit sets of every search.
For each heuristic and each task that it and blind search both solve with complete records, we form a \emph{pair} and compute its effort ratio $r$, unsplit ratio $u$ and fragmentation factor, and we call the pair \emph{harmful} if $r>1$, \emph{helpful} if $r<1$ and \emph{neutral} otherwise; all \NeutralPairs\ neutral pairs expand exactly the blind layers ($u=\fragfactor=1$).

\paragraph{Q1: Why Heuristics Hurt.}
Figure~\ref{fig:harm} and Table~\ref{tab:families} show that harmful heuristics barely prune but fragment.
Of the \num{\GenAllPairs} pairs of the family study, \num{\GenAllHarmful} are harmful, and on these pairs the median unsplit ratio lies between \GenMinHurtUnion\ and \GenMaxHurtUnion\ across the three families, whereas the median fragmentation factor lies between \GenMinHurtFrag\ and \GenMaxHurtFrag.
In contrast, on the \num{\GenAllHelpful} helpful pairs, the heuristic prunes strongly (median $u=\GenAllHelpUnion$) and fragments little (median \GenAllHelpFrag).
A post-hoc analysis of the PDB study shows the same pattern: harmful PDBs barely prune (median $u=\HarmHurtUnionMedian$) but fragment (median \HarmHurtFragMedian), and all \HarmDoubled\ pairs with $r>2$ have a fragmentation factor above two, while none has $u>2$.
In terms of coverage, no heuristic of the family study solves more tasks than blind search (\GenBlindSolved), and of the \UnsolvedFailed\ runs that fail on tasks that blind search solves, only \UnsolvedUnconstructed, all of M\&S, fail while constructing the heuristic.
Effort ratios predict ratios of total planner time well (Spearman correlation \RemEffTimeAllRho\ on the pairs of the remedy study, \RemEffTimeMinRho--\RemEffTimeMaxRho\ per family).

As a prespecified check, we asked that the share $\theta$ of fragmentation in the harmful effort, $\theta=\sum\ln\fragfactor/\sum\ln r$ over the harmful pairs, is at least \GenShareThreshold\ and that at least \GenStrongThresholdPercent\% of the pairs with $r>2$ have $\fragfactor>u$.
All three families meet it, and a post-hoc bootstrap that resamples domains gives 95\% intervals for $\theta$ above \GenShareThreshold\ for every family (lowest \BootPotShareLow--\BootPotShareHigh\ for potentials, pooled \BootAllShareLow--\BootAllShareHigh) and \BootAllUnionLow--\BootAllUnionHigh\ for the pooled median $u$ of harmful pairs.
The check is weak, however.
Since $\theta=1-\sum\ln u/\sum\ln r$, and since $r>2$ implies $\fragfactor>u$ whenever $u<\sqrt2$, it fails only if harmful heuristics substantially enlarge the expanded parts of the layers.
Likewise, since fragmentation exceeds one even on most helpful pairs, a heuristic that barely prunes is harmful almost by definition.
The substantive question is therefore why so many heuristics barely prune.

The answer is that BDDs shrink much less than the state sets they represent.
In a post-hoc analysis, we compare $u$ with the \emph{state ratio} $s$, the number of states that the heuristic search expands relative to blind search (Figure~\ref{fig:pruning}).
The unsplit ratio exceeds the state ratio on \PruneUnionAboveStates\ of the \PrunePairs\ pairs, and on most pairs of \PruneDomainsUnionAboveStates\ of the \PruneDomains\ domains.
Harmful PDBs and potentials remove few states at all (median $s=\PrunePdbHurtStates$ and \PrunePotHurtStates), and harmful M\&S heuristics remove a third of the states (median $s=\PruneMsHurtStates$) but leave the BDDs almost as large (median $u=\PruneMsHurtUnion$).
For the median harmful pair, \PruneHurtUnshrunk\% of the blind effort lies in layers whose expanded part has as many BDD nodes as the whole layer, versus \PruneHelpUnshrunk\% for helpful pairs, and \PruneHurtUnionAboveOne\ harmful pairs even have $u>1$, as Theorem~\ref{thm-effort} permits.
Fragmentation, in contrast, hardly depends on the state ratio (Spearman correlation \PruneSpearmanFrag, versus \PruneSpearmanUnion\ for $u$).

We chose the pair whose state ratio, unsplit ratio and fragmentation factor lie closest to the medians over all harmful M\&S pairs.
In the first \ExUnprunedLayers\ layers, the expanded part has exactly as many BDD nodes as the whole layer, but the heuristic splits it into up to \ExMaxBuckets\ buckets with up to \ExMaxUnprunedFrag\ times as many nodes.

Overall, the heuristic expands only \ExS\ times as many states as blind search, but its unsplit ratio is \ExU, and with fragmentation factor \ExFrag\ its effort ratio is \ExR.

Whether a heuristic pays off therefore depends mainly on how many states it removes: \PruneLowHelpful\ of the \PruneLowPairs\ pairs with $s<0.1$ are helpful, \the\numexpr\GenAllHelpful-\PruneLowHelpful-\PruneHighHelpful\relax\ of the \the\numexpr\GenAllPairs-\PruneLowPairs-\PruneHighPairs\relax\ pairs in between and only \PruneHighHelpful\ of the \PruneHighPairs\ pairs with $s\geq0.8$.

Stronger heuristics follow the same pattern.
Potentials optimized for all states solve \RemSolvedPotAllPlain\ tasks in the remedy study, more than our potentials (\RemSolvedPotPlain) and slightly but not significantly more than blind forward search (+\RemPotAllVsFwGains/$-$\RemPotAllVsFwLosses, unadjusted $p=0.37$).
Two further studies record these potentials (\num{\PotAllCells} runs) and two stronger configurations: potentials with weights in $[-1000,1000]$ optimized for all states and M\&S with \num{200000} states (\num{3156} runs).
All three pass the check of the family study (Table~\ref{tab:families}), and their pairs confirm the thresholds on $s$, which the first of these studies fixed in advance: \PotAllLowHelpful\ of the \PotAllLowPairs\ pairs with $s<0.1$ and \PotAllHighHelpful\ of the \PotAllHighPairs\ pairs with $s\geq0.8$ are helpful for the potentials optimized for all states.
These heuristics help on more pairs than our potentials (\PotAllHelpful, \StPotHelpful\ and \StMsHelpful, versus \GenPotHelpful), where they remove most states (median state ratio \PotAllHelpStates, \StPotHelpStates\ and \StMsHelpStates), whereas their harmful pairs barely prune (median $u$ \PotAllHurtUnion, \StPotHurtUnion\ and \StMsHurtUnion).
With weights up to 1000, the potentials solve \StPotSolved\ tasks, \the\numexpr\StPotSolved-\StBlindSolved\relax\ more than blind forward search in the same study (+\the\numexpr\StPotSolved-\StBlindSolved+\StPotLost\relax/$-$\StPotLost, unadjusted $p=0.03$, post hoc), whereas M\&S with \num{200000} states solves only \StMsSolved, since \StMsUnconstructed\ of its \StMsLost\ failures on tasks that blind search solves occur while it constructs the heuristic (post hoc).

\paragraph{Q2: Heuristics without Fragmentation.}
A search that knows \copt, expands the layers by increasing $g$ and removes states with $f>\copt$ without splitting the layers expands exactly the sets $S_g^h$ (Lemma~\ref{lem-identity}), so its effort ratio is $u$.
In a post-hoc comparison, such an oracle search would expand fewer BDD nodes than blind search on \OracleAllUnsplitBelow\ of the \num{\GenAllPairs} pairs of the family study, compared with \OracleAllEffortBelow\ for \bddastar.
The geometric mean of $u$ is \OracleAllGeoU\ versus \OracleAllGeoR\ for $r$ (M\&S \OracleMsGeoU\ versus \OracleMsGeoR, PDB \OraclePdbGeoU\ versus \OraclePdbGeoR, potentials \OraclePotGeoU\ versus \OraclePotGeoR).
Forward search, however, learns \copt\ only when it finds a plan.
Three further studies therefore compare practical searches that avoid fragmentation with \bddastar\ and blind forward search, each on the holdout set with its own runs of both and with Holm correction over its own sign tests (Table~\ref{tab:remedy}).
\emph{Prune-only} search expands the blind layers whole and uses the heuristic only to remove dead ends.
\emph{Batched} search keeps the order of \bddastar\ but computes the successors of buckets with the same $g$-value and $f$-values at most one apart as one BDD if their union has no more nodes than its parts.
\emph{Unsplit} search runs iterative deepening on an $f$-bound $\beta$ that starts at $h(\sinit)$: each iteration expands the layers by increasing $g$, each as one BDD, and removes states with $g+h>\beta$, so the first plan it finds is optimal whenever $\beta\geq\copt$; otherwise, the next iteration starts from scratch with $\beta$ set to the smallest removed $f$-value plus a step that starts at one and doubles in every iteration.
\emph{Bounded} search first runs LAMA \citep{richter-westphal-jair2010} for at most 30 seconds and then prune-only search that removes states whose $f$-value is at least the cost of LAMA's plan, so it either finds a cheaper plan, which is optimal, or proves LAMA's plan optimal.

Without a bound, avoiding fragmentation does not make the heuristics pay off.
Prune-only search recovers the losses of potentials (+\RemPotPruneVsPlainGains/$-$\RemPotPruneVsPlainLosses\ against \bddastar) but expands as many BDD nodes as blind search (median ratio \RemPotPruneEffortRemedy), since it removes dead ends on at most \RemPhMaxPruneDeadEnds\ tasks.
Batching leaves the expanded buckets unchanged, and unsplit search spends more on earlier iterations and on overshooting \copt\ than it saves: the earlier iterations account for a median of \the\numexpr100-\UbMaxLastShare\relax--\the\numexpr100-\UbMinLastShare\relax\% of its effort, and its median effort ratio to blind search exceeds that of \bddastar\ for every heuristic (potentials: \UbPotEffortUnsplit\ versus \UbPotEffortPlain).
With the bound of LAMA, the heuristics no longer hurt: bounded search solves significantly more tasks than \bddastar\ with the PDB (+\BdPdbVsPlainGains/$-$\BdPdbVsPlainLosses) and potentials (+\BdPotVsPlainGains/$-$\BdPotVsPlainLosses) and slightly but not significantly more than blind forward search with every heuristic.
The bound, however, does most of the work: blind search with the same bound solves \BdSolvedBlindBounded\ tasks, and no bounded heuristic search differs significantly from it.
LAMA's plans cost a median of \BdBoundExcessMin--\BdBoundExcessMax\% more than optimal, so pruning with them removes few states, and the median effort ratio of bounded search to blind search is \BdPotEffortBounded\ for every heuristic.
Pruning thus pays off only with bounds much closer to \copt.
Gains of a few tasks over blind forward search need not come from the heuristic: on tasks where prune-only search expands the same BDDs as blind forward search, SymK's heuristic search engine needs a median of only \RemPhPdbPruneImageTime--\RemPhMsPruneImageTime\ times its image time (post hoc).
Blind bidirectional search solves \RemSolvedBlindBd\ tasks, \RemBdVsFwGains\ more than blind forward search and none fewer.

\paragraph{Q3: Operator Potentials in GHSETA*.}
A further study runs the planner CPDDL of \citet{fiser-et-al-aij2024} on the holdout set: blind forward and bidirectional search and the same searches with operator potentials for the average state with the value of the initial state kept maximal (A+I), in the forward direction of GHSETA*, the configuration recommended for CPDDL.
Separate forward runs log the expanded sets and their per-layer unions (\num{6312} runs in total; CPDDL cannot parse three of the domains).
Here the heuristic pays off: forward search solves \CpSolvedPotFw\ instead of \CpSolvedBlindFw\ tasks (+\CpFwGains/$-$\CpFwLosses) and bidirectional search \CpSolvedPotBi\ instead of \CpSolvedBlindBi\ (+\CpBiGains/$-$\CpBiLosses), both significantly after Holm correction.
The decomposition shows the pattern of Q1 (Table~\ref{tab:families}, A+I row): the potentials help on \CpHelpful\ of \CpPairs\ pairs, where they remove most states (median state ratio \CpHelpStates) and prune strongly (median $u=\CpHelpUnion$) while fragmenting little (\CpHelpFrag), and they hurt on \CpHarmful\ pairs, which barely prune (median $u=\CpHurtUnion$, state ratio \CpHurtStates) but fragment (\CpHurtFrag).
As in SymK, whether they help depends on how many states they remove (\CpLowHelpful\ of the \CpLowPairs\ pairs with $s<0.1$ are helpful, but only \CpHighHelpful\ of the \CpHighPairs\ pairs with $s\geq0.8$).
The decomposition does not explain, however, why operator potentials gain so many more tasks: the SymK potentials with weights up to 1000 decompose almost identically (Table~\ref{tab:families}) but gain only \the\numexpr\StPotSolved-\StBlindSolved\relax\ tasks over blind search.
We conjecture that the difference lies in factors that the decomposition does not capture, such as the time per expanded node, the encoding of the task or the weaker blind baseline of CPDDL.
A second CPDDL study changes only the objective to the initial state, as for our potentials (\num{4208} runs).
These potentials still beat blind forward search (\CpISolvedPotFw\ versus \CpISolvedBlindFw, +\CpIGains/$-$\CpILosses, significant), but they help on only \CpIHelpful\ of \CpIPairs\ pairs, and their harmful pairs do not prune at all (median $u=\CpIHurtUnion$) but fragment (\CpIHurtFrag); only \CpIStrongFrag\ of their \CpIStrong\ pairs with $r>2$ have $\fragfactor>u$, slightly fewer than the \GenStrongThresholdPercent\% of our check.
The objective thus matters, but even these potentials gain more in CPDDL than in SymK.
Like Q1, the decomposition covers only tasks that both searches solve, whereas the coverage gains come from tasks that only the heuristic search solves.

\paragraph{Q4: Tightness of the Bounds.}
No observed fragmentation exceeds its bounds on any of the \num{\SlackObservations} solved and completely recorded runs of the PDB study.
The heuristics of the family study are far from the worst case: their median widths range from \WidthMedianMin\ to \WidthMedianMax\ with \ValuesMedianMin--\ValuesMedianMax\ values on tasks with a median of \BitsMedianMin--\BitsMedianMax\ state bits, so $2\values Wn$, with the number of ADD values for $\values$, exceeds their observed fragmentation by a median factor of \WidthSlackMedian\ and at least \WidthSlackMin\ (post hoc).
In a post-hoc analysis of the PDB study, the median factor by which a bound exceeds the observed fragmentation is \SlackProfileMedian\ for the profile bound and \SlackWidthMedian\ for its relaxation to width.
However, the bounds do not predict fragmentation.
The profile bound orders the PDBs of a task by fragmentation with \emph{concordance}, the fraction of correctly ordered pairs averaged over domains, of \HoldoutDenominatorProfileMacro, but $1/\unioneffort$, which shares its denominator, already reaches \HoldoutDenominatorNullMacro\ on the same pairs, and width alone is as good as the profile (supplement).
When only the variable order of one PDB changes, the profile bound predicts the effect of switching from the Gamer to the Fast Downward order (concordance \OrderProfileMacro), but not among the Gamer orders of four unseen random seeds (\SeedCartesianMacro, below the \SeedNullMacro\ of $1/\unioneffort$).
Likewise, aligning the M\&S merge order with the BDD order, which bounds its width, changes fragmentation by a geometric mean factor of only \PruneAlignedFragRatio\ on \PruneAlignedTasks\ tasks (post hoc).
Nor does width yield better PDBs: no width-limited PDB selector, with or without capped values, solves more tasks than blind search (supplement).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Pairs cover only tasks that both searches solve, so we cannot decompose the \PruneBlindOnlyMin--\PruneBlindOnlyMax\ tasks per heuristic configuration that only blind search solves.
On these \UnsolvedRuns\ runs, the heuristic search had already expanded a median of \UnsolvedEffortMedian\ times the whole blind effort when it ran out of time, and in \UnsolvedLayersAbove\ of the \UnsolvedLayers\ layers it had begun (\UnsolvedLayersPercent\%), its buckets already had more BDD nodes than the whole blind layer, which only fragmentation or enlarged subsets can cause (post hoc).
We exclude tasks with zero-cost operators, which Lemma~\ref{lem-identity} does not cover, and our bounds and SymK studies concern forward product-at-evaluation search with one variable order per task; symbolic PDBs as in Gamer, the perimeter abstractions of \symba\ \citep{torralba-et-al-ijcai2016a,torralba-et-al-aij2018} and heuristics in the backward direction remain open.
We designed the later studies after seeing the results of the earlier ones on the same holdout tasks, so only the analyses within each study are prospective.
Coverage near the time limit of 300 seconds is noisy: blind forward search of SymK solves between \RemSolvedBlindFw\ and \StBlindSolved\ holdout tasks in different studies, so we compare configurations only within a study.

\section{Related Work}

\citet{edelkamp-kissmann-aaai2008} and \citet{kissmann-phd2012} derive polynomial upper and exponential lower bounds on the BDD sizes of the layers of blind symbolic search on planning benchmarks, whereas we bound heuristic search relative to blind search on the same task.
Guarantees for symbolic versus explicit search concern transition relations and goal formulas and explicitly leave heuristics aside \citep{speck-helmert-ecai2025}.
Residual width, products of cofactors and Apply are standard \citep{wegener-2000,bryant-ieeecomp1986}; new are their connection to the buckets of symbolic search, the cumulative bounds relative to blind search and the matching planning family.

\citet{edelkamp-aips2002} computes PDBs symbolically, and the planner Gamer guides \bddastar\ with symbolic PDBs \citep{kissmann-edelkamp-aaai2011,edelkamp-et-al-aaai2015}.
PDBs, pattern selection and their symbolic computation are well studied \citep{culberson-schaeffer-compint1998,edelkamp-ecp2001,haslum-et-al-aaai2007,rovner-et-al-icaps2019,edelkamp-et-al-ecai2012}.
\citet{fiser-et-al-aaai2022,fiser-et-al-aij2024} use operator-potential heuristics in \ghseta, which expands the same buckets as \bddastar\ but computes $h$ by splitting the transition relation.
Compared with blind forward search, their best potentials expand more BDDs, each smaller on average, and often fewer nodes in total \citep{fiser-et-al-aaai2022}; we separate these effects into pruning and fragmentation and show that the same decomposition holds for heuristics that do not pay off.
\citet{seipp-et-al-icaps2015} compare optimization functions for potential heuristics in explicit search.
Prune-only search resembles the pruning of blind symbolic search with $h^2$ mutexes \citep{torralba-et-al-aij2017}, with dead ends of a heuristic instead of mutexes.
ADDA\ensuremath{^{*}} \citep{hansen-et-al-sara2002} instead maps states to their values in one ADD, which by the argument of Theorem~\ref{thm-bucket} has at most $\cutprod{S}{h}$ nodes for a set $S$ and thus avoids the factor \values\ of Theorem~\ref{thm-effort}.
Symbolic breadth-first heuristic search \citep{jensen-et-al-icaps2006} expands unsplit layers and prunes them with an upper bound on the plan cost, as our unsplit search does with its iterated bound.
Partitioned BDDs in verification likewise represent a function by several BDDs over a partition of its inputs, which can be smaller or larger than one BDD \citep{narayan-et-al-iccad1996}.

% ============================ conclusions ================================
\section{Conclusions}

We separated the two effects of a heuristic in forward symbolic \astar\ by factoring the number of expanded BDD nodes into unsplit effort and fragmentation.
In SymK, PDB, M\&S and potential heuristics that more than doubled the expanded BDD nodes barely pruned but fragmented the layers, since their BDDs shrank much less than the state sets they represented.
Heuristics therefore paid off mainly where they removed most states, for stronger heuristics and for operator potentials in GHSETA* as well, although the decomposition did not explain why operator potentials gained more tasks than strong potentials in SymK.
Pruning without fragmenting would pay off far more often, but iterative deepening on the bound cost more than it saved, and the plan costs of LAMA removed the harm of the heuristics without enabling much pruning.
Cofactor width bounds fragmentation, pruned sets and the effort relative to blind search, and the bounds on fragmentation and effort are tight up to a constant factor in the worst case, but they do not predict fragmentation in practice.
Heuristics could therefore pay off much more often in forward symbolic search if a cheap and tight bound on the optimal plan cost were available.
